Because there are two completely different strategies for connecting with the world. Both work brilliantly. And understanding the difference is where this gets really fun.

Two Ways to Connect the World

Imagine two ways to throw a party.

Party A (US, UK, Canada, Australia): A new hit every week. Everyone dances for a month, then moves to the next song. The DJ never stops dropping fresh tracks.

Party B (Puerto Rico, Colombia): Fewer songs, but they become anthems. People play them all summer. They become the soundtrack of an entire season.

How Countries Play Different Roles

Not every country participates the same way in this global music exchange. Some broadcast. Some receive. Some build walls. Some build bridges to their neighbors.

Here are the patterns we found:

● The Global Broadcasters

The US, UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and Ireland form an open network. They share music freely among themselves and broadcast it relentlessly to the world.

Their superpower is freshness. Over half their exports are songs released that same year. The new-music pipeline never stops. Your Discover Weekly? Mostly these countries talking to you.

● The Self-Sufficient Scenes

Some countries have such a rich local music scene that imported songs barely crack their charts. Their own artists fill the room.

These aren't isolated -- they simply produce enough incredible music to fill their own playlists first. Brazil's funk carioca, Korea's K-pop, France's rap scene. They don't need the import aisle because the local produce is that good.

● The Regional Bridges

Some countries don't broadcast globally. Instead, they build musical bridges to their neighbors.

Puerto Rico → Latin America. Music flows to Mexico, Argentina, Chile, Colombia. The bridge is built on shared language and rhythm.

South Korea → Southeast Asia. 23% of Thailand's imports. 28% of Vietnam's. K-pop travels a very specific route.

Turkey → Central Asia & Middle East. Cultural familiarity creates its own highway.

This is maybe the most beautiful finding: music doesn't just spread randomly. It follows cultural bonds. Shared history, shared language, shared feeling. The algorithm doesn't decide what connects us -- proximity and culture do.

Why Latin Music Connects Differently

Latin artists dominate the "top artist" ranking while English songs dominate the "top song" ranking. Here's why that's not a contradiction -- it's a completely different approach to connection.

1. They build together

In the US, 40% of exported songs feature multiple artists. In Puerto Rico and Colombia? 72%. Features, duets, "ft." -- every collaboration introduces one artist's audience to another.

2. Their songs become anthems

A US hit stays on foreign charts for about 11 weeks. A Colombian hit? 30 weeks. These songs don't fade -- they become summer soundtracks, party staples, shared memories.

3. One name carries a nation

The US exports through 2,845 artists. Puerto Rico through 456 -- and Bad Bunny alone is 42% of it. That's not a weakness. It means one artist becomes a cultural ambassador so powerful that an entire country's identity travels on their name.

So Is Your Music Taste Unique?

In some ways, yes. Nobody else has your exact combination of songs, memories, and 2 a.m. playlists.

But the raw materials? They were shared with millions of people across dozens of countries. The same melodies that moved you moved someone in Seoul, someone in Bogota, someone in Lagos.

That's not a flaw. That's the most human thing about music: it connects us without asking permission. Across languages, borders, and time zones.

Your playlist is yours. But it was built from pieces that belong to the whole world.

How We Measured "Music Trade"

  • Weekly charts: We summed daily streams into weekly totals and kept the top 100 songs per country per week (3.16 million records).
  • Artist nationality: We mapped 84,985 artists to their home countries using Wikipedia, Wikidata, MusicBrainz, and other public sources. Coverage: 99.9%.
  • Export = charting abroad: When a song charts in a country that isn't the artist's home, that's an "export" for the artist's country and an "import" for the listening country.
  • Every market counts equally: A chart-week in Luxembourg = a chart-week in the US. This measures cultural reach, not commercial revenue.
  • 17 analytical lenses per country: volume, diversity, longevity, spread, velocity, collaboration, dependency, chart quality, freshness, concentration, and cultural blocs.
