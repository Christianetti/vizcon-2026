The World Listens to Imports

Think of your country's music charts as a shelf in a store. Some of the products are made locally. Most? Shipped in from somewhere else.

Globally, only 33% of what people listen to comes from their own country. The rest -- two out of every three songs on your charts -- was made somewhere else, by someone who has never set foot in your city.

Two Ways to Conquer the World

Imagine two restaurants.

Restaurant A (the US, UK, Canada, Australia) opens a new location every week. Each one is packed for a month, then a new one opens and everyone moves there. Hundreds of locations, brief stays, maximum footprint.

Restaurant B (Puerto Rico, Colombia) opens fewer locations. But people come back every single week for months. The menu does not change. It does not need to.

That is the difference between Anglophone and Latin music exports.

Their strategy: volume and freshness. You heard that song last month? It is already being replaced. Your Discover Weekly is full of these. The owl knows.

● The Cultural Fortresses

Some countries looked at the global music flood and said: no, thank you.

The owl respects a closed door that stays closed. These nations produce enough music to feed themselves. They do not need the import aisle.

● The Regional Corridors

Some countries do not export globally. They export to their neighbors.

Puerto Rico sends music to 15+ Latin American countries. Not to Finland. Not to Japan. The corridor is linguistic and cultural.

South Korea dominates Southeast Asia: 23% of Thailand's imports, 28% of Vietnam's. K-pop travels on a very specific route.

Turkey sends music across Central Asia and the Middle East. Not to Scandinavia.

Cultural proximity matters more than Spotify's algorithm. Your neighbors shape your charts more than any recommendation engine.

The Latin Secret

The owl promised to explain why Latin artists dominate the rankings while Latin songs do not win the "most traveled" award. It comes down to three things:

1. They collaborate constantly

In the US, 40% of exported songs feature multiple artists. In Puerto Rico and Colombia? 72%.

When Bad Bunny features Feid, Colombia listens. When Feid features Bad Bunny, Puerto Rico listens. It compounds.

2. Their songs do not expire

A US hit cycles out of charts in 11 weeks (median). A Colombian hit stays for 30. A Puerto Rican hit for 26.

The songs are built to last: danceable, repeatable, tied to moments rather than trends.

3. Fewer artists carry more weight

The US exports through 2,845 different artists. Puerto Rico through 456 -- and Bad Bunny alone is 42% of it.

This is not a weakness. It means each artist builds a personal brand so strong that an entire country's musical identity travels on their name.

References & Methodology

Data Source

Spotify Charts Daily (2017-2025)
Public dataset: gonzalopezgil/spotify-charts-daily-updated (Kaggle)

  • 42.8 million rows
  • 70 countries (Israel and Belarus excluded due to data gaps)
  • Daily top-200 songs per country with stream counts

Methodology

  • Weekly aggregation: Daily charts summed to weekly by streams, ranked top 100 per country per week. This gives us 3.16 million song-country-week records.
  • Artist nationality: Each of the 84,985 artists mapped to 1-2 home countries using Wikipedia, Wikidata, MusicBrainz, Kaggle datasets, and language-model-assisted research. Coverage: 99.9%.
  • Trade classification: When a song charts in the artist's home country, it is "local." When it charts anywhere else, it is an "import" for that country and an "export" for the artist's home country. Each chart-week = 1 unit of cultural influence, regardless of country size.
  • Dual-nationality artists (e.g., born in one country, based in another) are counted as local in both home countries. Export credit is split equally.
  • 17 analytical lenses applied per country: export volume, artist/song diversity, longevity, spread, velocity, collaboration patterns, market dependency, chart position quality, freshness, artist concentration, and cultural bloc membership.
  • Country clustering: Standardized scores across metrics to identify behavioral archetypes.

What "Export Weeks" Means

Every time a song appears in the top 100 of a country that is NOT the artist's home country, that counts as 1 export week. We treat every market equally -- a chart-week in Luxembourg counts the same as a chart-week in the United States. This measures cultural reach, not commercial revenue.
