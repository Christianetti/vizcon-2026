Here is something wonderful: when you press play on Spotify, you are almost certainly listening to someone from a different country. Not sometimes. Most of the time. Across 70 countries and nine years of chart data, two-thirds of what we stream was made somewhere else.

That means music is doing what diplomacy, trade deals, and social media only dream of. It is connecting us. Quietly, joyfully, without anyone needing a visa.

Let us look at how.

The Song That Went Everywhere

Every year, one song travels farther than any other. It accumulates "foreign chart-weeks" by appearing on Top 200 charts in countries outside its home. The all-time champion?

Blinding Lights by The Weeknd: 7,051 foreign chart-weeks across 67 countries.

The Artist Who Changed Everything

Songs are sprints. Artists are marathons. And when we zoom out from individual tracks to entire catalogs, the picture flips completely.

Bad Bunny: 126,075 foreign chart-weeks. More than double the #2 artist.

The Paradox: Width vs. Depth

So English songs win at spread (appearing in the most countries) while Latin artists win at longevity (staying on charts the longest). Two completely different strategies for connecting across borders.

The numbers tell the story clearly. A typical English-language export lasts about 11 weeks on a foreign chart, reaching 3 countries on average. A typical Latin export? 30 weeks, reaching 5 countries. Half the spread, triple the staying power.

English music goes everywhere briefly. Latin music goes somewhere and stays forever.

Think of it this way: an Ed Sheeran track might appear on 58 charts for a few months each. A Bad Bunny track might only reach 20 charts, but it stays for over half a year on each one. Both strategies generate massive foreign chart-weeks. They just do it differently.

Three Ways Countries Connect

Not every country participates in this global music economy the same way. We can group them into three broad patterns:

Global Broadcasters

The US and UK are the superpowers here. The US alone accounts for 772,650 foreign chart-weeks across our dataset, with 76% of its own chart being domestic artists. The UK contributes 251,851 export-weeks. These countries produce music that goes everywhere, and their home audiences mainly listen to their own output. They broadcast outward.

Self-Sufficient Scenes

Japan (72.5% local) and South Korea (71.6% local) have huge domestic markets that primarily consume homegrown music. But unlike the US and UK, their exports are more concentrated: K-pop travels through dedicated fanbases to specific markets, while J-pop mostly stays regional. They are self-sufficient but increasingly leaking outward.

Regional Bridges

Colombia is the perfect example. With 142,004 export-weeks, it punches way above its weight by serving as the bridge to all of Latin America and increasingly the world. Its music reaches a median of 5 countries per export and stays for 30 weeks. Colombia does not just make music for itself. It makes music for an entire linguistic region, and that region is enormous.

Why Latin Music Dominates

Three factors explain why Latin artists accumulate so many more foreign chart-weeks than anyone else:

1. Collaboration Culture

Puerto Rico's export tracks have a 63.8% collaboration rate. Colombia's is 58.4%. Compare that to the UK (32.9%) or South Korea (22.1%). When every song features two or three artists, each one brings their audience along. Collabs are not just creative choices. They are distribution strategies.

2. Extraordinary Longevity

A Latin export stays on foreign charts for a median of 24 to 30 weeks. An English-language export: 10 to 11 weeks. A K-pop export: 9 weeks. Latin music does not burn bright and fade. It smolders for months.

3. Concentration

Bad Bunny alone accounts for 22.9% of all Puerto Rico's export-weeks. One artist, nearly a quarter of an entire territory's global presence. This kind of concentration means that when the top artist releases an album, the whole country's chart presence surges worldwide.

Collaboration, longevity, star power. Three ingredients that turned reggaeton into the world's most persistent musical export.

Methodology

Data sourced from Spotify's Weekly Top 200 charts across approximately 70 countries, spanning January 2016 through June 2025. A "foreign chart-week" is defined as one week where a track appears on a Top 200 chart outside its artist's home country. Artist origins were determined by Spotify metadata and manual verification. Country-level metrics (longevity, spread, openness) are computed from the full 9-year panel.
